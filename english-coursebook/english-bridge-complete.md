## Grammar

### Present Simple vs Present Continuous

**Present Simple** is for:
- Habits and routines: *I drink coffee every morning.*
- General truths: *The sun rises in the east.*
- Permanent situations: *She works in a hospital.*

**Present Continuous** is for:
- Actions happening now: *I am drinking coffee right now.*
- Temporary situations: *He is staying with his parents this month.*
- Plans for the near future: *We are meeting friends tonight.*

| Present Simple | Present Continuous |
|----------------|-------------------|
| I **work** every day. | I **am working** right now. |
| She **catches** the bus at 8 am. | She **is catching** the bus at the moment. |
| They **live** in London. | They **are living** in London this year. |

### Stative Verbs

Some verbs describe a state, not an action. We do NOT usually use them in continuous forms.

Common stative verbs: **know, like, love, hate, want, need, believe, understand**

- Correct: *I **want** a new phone.*
- Incorrect: *I am wanting a new phone.*
- Correct: *She **likes** chocolate.*
- Incorrect: *She is liking chocolate.*

> ### Common Mistakes
>
> ❌ *I am going to the gym every day.*
> ✅ *I go to the gym every day.* (habit → present simple)
>
> ❌ *He is knowing the answer.*
> ✅ *He knows the answer.* (stative verb → present simple)
>
> ❌ *She works at the moment.*
> ✅ *She is working at the moment.* (now → present continuous)

## Pronunciation

### Third Person -s: /s/, /z/, /ɪz/

The letter -s at the end of verbs in present simple (he/she/it) has three different sounds.

| Sound | When? | Examples |
|-------|-------|----------|
| /s/ | after voiceless sounds (f, k, p, t, th) | **talks** /tɔːks/, **makes** /meɪks/, **sleeps** /sliːps/ |
| /z/ | after voiced sounds and vowels (b, d, g, l, m, n, r, v, all vowels) | **lives** /lɪvz/, **goes** /ɡəʊz/, **reads** /riːdz/ |
| /ɪz/ | after s, sh, ch, x, z sounds | **watches** /wɒtʃɪz/, **finishes** /fɪnɪʃɪz/, **relaxes** /rɪlæksɪz/ |

**Practice:** Say these words aloud with the correct sound.

- /s/: **wakes, eats, likes, drinks, takes**
- /z/: **lives, does, goes, knows, buys**
- /ɪz/: **teaches, washes, fixes, relaxes, catches**

> **Tip:** Put your hand on your throat. For /z/ you feel a vibration. For /s/ you do not.

## Grammar

### Past Simple vs Past Continuous

**Past Simple** is for:
- Completed actions in the past: *I visited Paris last year.*
- A sequence of events: *She woke up, had breakfast, and left.*
- Past states: *He lived in New York for five years.*

**Past Continuous** is for:
- Actions in progress at a specific past time: *At 8 pm, I was watching a movie.*
- Interrupted actions (with *when*): *I was sleeping when the phone rang.*
- Background description in stories: *The sun was shining, and the birds were singing.*

| Past Simple | Past Continuous |
|-------------|-----------------|
| I **traveled** to Japan. | I **was traveling** to Japan when I got sick. |
| She **arrived** at 6 pm. | She **was arriving** when the airport closed. |
| They **ate** dinner. | They **were eating** dinner when the power went out. |

**When + Past Simple** interrupts the **Past Continuous** action:

- *I **was taking** a shower when the doorbell **rang**.*
- *They **were driving** home when it **started** to rain.*

> ### Common Mistakes
>
> ❌ *I was going to the beach yesterday.*
> ✅ *I went to the beach yesterday.* (completed action → past simple)
>
> ❌ *When I was arriving, the taxi was waiting.*
> ✅ *When I arrived, the taxi was waiting.* (arrival = completed action)
>
> ❌ *While he cooked dinner, I was reading.*
> ✅ *While he was cooking dinner, I was reading.* (two simultaneous ongoing actions)
>
> ❌ *She enjoyed the trip while she was in Italy.*
> ✅ *She enjoyed the trip while she was in Italy.* (both can work, but with *enjoy* as a state, past simple is more natural)

| Sound | When? | Examples |
|-------|-------|----------|

## Grammar

### Present Perfect for Life Experiences

We use the present perfect to talk about experiences in our life. We do not say exactly when they happened.

**Form:** have / has + past participle

- *I **have visited** Thailand.*
- *She **has eaten** insects.*
- *They **have never flown** in a helicopter.*

**Questions:** Have / Has + subject + past participle

- ***Have** you **ever tried** Mexican food?*
- ***Has** he **ever been** to Australia?*

**Short answers:**

- *Yes, I have. / No, I haven't.*
- *Yes, he has. / No, he hasn't.*

### Been vs Gone

| **Been** (= went and returned) | **Gone** (= went and is still there) |
|--------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| *She **has been** to Paris.* (She went and came back.) | *She **has gone** to Paris.* (She is in Paris now.) |
| *I **have been** to the supermarket.* (I am back home.) | *He **has gone** to the supermarket.* (He is at the supermarket now.) |

### Present Perfect vs Past Simple

| Present Perfect | Past Simple |
|-----------------|-------------|
| No specific time | Specific time |
| *I **have visited** London.* (at some point in my life) | *I **visited** London **last year.*** (specific time) |
| *She **has met** a famous person.* | *She **met** a famous person **in 2019.*** |
| *They **have never tried** Thai food.* | *They **never tried** Thai food **when they were in Thailand.*** |

### Sentence Stress in Present Perfect Questions

## Grammar

### Countable and Uncountable Nouns

**Countable nouns** — things you can count. They have a singular and plural form.

- *one apple, two apples, three apples*
- *an egg, some eggs*

**Uncountable nouns** — things you cannot count easily. They have only one form.

- *water, rice, bread, milk, sugar, cheese, information, advice*
- ❌ *two waters* — ✅ *two glasses of water*

### A/An, Some, Any

| | Countable | Uncountable |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| **Positive** | *There is **an** orange.* / *There are **some** oranges.* | *There is **some** milk.* |
| **Negative** | *There aren't **any** oranges.* | *There isn't **any** milk.* |
| **Question** | *Are there **any** oranges?* | *Is there **any** milk?* |

Use **a/an** with singular countable nouns. Use **some** in positive sentences. Use **any** in questions and negatives.

### Much, Many, A Lot Of

| | Countable | Uncountable |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| Positive | *There are **a lot of** apples.* | *There is **a lot of** rice.* |
| Question | ***How many** apples are there?* | ***How much** rice is there?* |
| Negative | *There aren't **many** apples.* | *There isn't **much** rice.* |

**Many** = countable. **Much** = uncountable. **A lot of** = both (positive sentences).

### A Few, A Little

| | Countable | Meaning |
|---|-----------|---------|
| **a few** | apples | a small number |
| **a little** | rice | a small amount |

- *I have **a few** friends in this city.* (a small number, but enough)
- *I have **a little** money left.* (a small amount, but enough)

### How Much / How Many

- ***How many** eggs do we need?* (countable → answer: *three eggs*)
- ***How much** sugar do you want?* (uncountable → answer: *a little*)

> ### Common Mistakes
>
> ❌ *I need an information.*
> ✅ *I need some information.* (information is uncountable)
>
> ❌ *How much apples do you want?*
> ✅ *How many apples do you want?* (apples are countable)
>
> ❌ *I don't have some money.*
> ✅ *I don't have any money.* (negative → use *any*)
>
> ❌ *There aren't much people here.*
> ✅ *There aren't many people here.* (people is countable)

## Grammar

### Present Perfect with *For* and *Since*

We use the present perfect to talk about actions or situations that started in the past and continue to now.

**Form:** have / has + past participle

- *I **have worked** at this company for three years.*
- *She **has lived** in London since 2020.*

**For** = a period of time (duration)

| Expression | Example |
|------------|---------|
| for three years | *He has worked here **for three years.*** |
| for a long time | *I have known her **for a long time.*** |
| for six months | *They have been married **for six months.*** |

**Since** = a starting point in time

| Expression | Example |
|------------|---------|
| since 2019 | *She has worked here **since 2019.*** |
| since March | *He has been a manager **since March.*** |
| since I was a child | *I have wanted to be a nurse **since I was a child.*** |

**Questions and short answers:**

- *How long **have** you **worked** here?* — *I **have worked** here for two years.*
- *How long **has** she **had** that job?* — *She **has had** it since January.*

### Modal Verbs: Obligation, Prohibition, and Advice

| Modal Verb | Meaning | Example |
|------------|---------|---------|
| **have to** | obligation (necessary) | *I **have to** finish this report by Friday.* |
| **don't have to** | no obligation (not necessary) | *You **don't have to** wear a suit to work.* |
| **must** | strong obligation (very important) | *Employees **must** arrive on time.* |
| **mustn't** | prohibition (not allowed) | *You **mustn't** use your phone in the meeting.* |
| **should** | advice or recommendation | *You **should** update your CV every year.* |

**Have to vs Must:**

- *Have to* is more common in everyday English. It describes external rules or needs.
- *Must* is stronger and more formal. It often describes rules from the speaker or the organization.

**Don't have to vs Mustn't:**

- *Don't have to* = it is not necessary (you can choose).
- *Mustn't* = it is prohibited (you cannot do it).

- *You **don't have to** work on Saturdays.* (It is optional.)
- *You **mustn't** share company passwords.* (It is against the rules.)

> ### Common Mistakes
>
> ❌ *I have worked here since three years.*
> ✅ *I have worked here for three years.* (period of time → *for*)
>
> ❌ *She works here since 2021.*
> ✅ *She has worked here since 2021.* (continuing action → present perfect)
>
> ❌ *You don't must arrive late.*
> ✅ *You mustn't arrive late.* (negative of *must* is *mustn't*)
>
> ❌ *He has to works on Saturdays.*
> ✅ *He has to work on Saturdays.* (after *have to*, use base verb)
>
> ❌ *I have 30 years old.*
> ✅ *I am 30 years old.* (age → verb *to be*)

### Weak Forms of *Have* and *To* in Present Perfect

In present perfect sentences, the words *have* and *to* are usually weak and unstressed. We say them quickly and quietly.

## Grammar

### Comparative and Superlative Adjectives

We use **comparatives** to compare two things. We use **superlatives** to compare three or more things.

| Type | Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|------|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| One syllable | fast | faster | the fastest |
| One syllable | cheap | cheaper | the cheapest |
| One syllable | big | bigger | the biggest |
| One syllable | slow | slower | the slowest |
| Two syllables ending in -y | easy | easier | the easiest |
| Two syllables ending in -y | happy | happier | the happiest |
| Two syllables (other) | modern | more modern | the most modern |
| Two syllables (other) | useful | more useful | the most useful |
| Three+ syllables | expensive | more expensive | the most expensive |
| Three+ syllables | interesting | more interesting | the most interesting |

**Spelling rules:**
- One syllable ending in consonant + vowel + consonant → double the last letter: *big → bigger, hot → hotter*
- Two syllables ending in -y → change -y to -ier: *easy → easier, happy → happier*
- Two+ syllables (not ending in -y) → add *more / most*: *expensive → more expensive → the most expensive*

**Examples:**
- *This phone is **cheaper** than that one.* (comparative)
- *This is **the cheapest** phone in the store.* (superlative)
- *My laptop is **more powerful** than yours.*
- *This is **the most powerful** laptop on the market.*

### Irregular Forms

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| good | better | the best |
| bad | worse | the worst |
| far | farther / further | the farthest / the furthest |

- *This app is **better** than the old one.*
- *That was **the worst** movie I have ever seen.*
- *My house is **farther** from the office than yours.*

### (Not) As ... As

We use **as + adjective + as** to say two things are equal. We use **not as + adjective + as** to say they are not equal.

- *The new model is **as fast as** the old one.* (equal)
- *This tablet is **not as expensive as** a laptop.* (the tablet is cheaper)
- *My phone is **as good as** yours.* (equal)
- *Streaming is **not as cheap as** downloading.* (streaming is more expensive)

### Comparative Adverbs

We also use comparative forms with adverbs to compare actions.

| Adverb | Comparative |
|--------|-------------|
| fast | faster |
| quickly | more quickly |
| carefully | more carefully |
| well | better |
| badly | worse |

- *This phone runs **faster** than my old one.*
- *She types **more quickly** than I do.*
- *He works **more carefully** now.*
- *This app works **better** than the previous version.*

> ### Common Mistakes
>
> ❌ *This phone is more cheap than that one.*
> ✅ *This phone is cheaper than that one.* (one syllable → add -er)
>
> ❌ *This is the goodest phone I have used.*
> ✅ *This is the best phone I have used.* (irregular: good → better → the best)
>
> ❌ *My laptop is more big than yours.*
> ✅ *My laptop is bigger than yours.* (one syllable, double consonant)
>
> ❌ *This tablet is not as expensive like a laptop.*
> ✅ *This tablet is not as expensive as a laptop.* (use *as...as*, not *as...like*)
>
> ❌ *She speaks English gooder than me.*
> ✅ *She speaks English better than me.* (irregular: well → better)

## Grammar

### Future Forms: *Will*, *Going To*, Present Continuous

We use different forms to talk about the future. The form you choose depends on the meaning.

#### *Will* + base verb

| Use | Example |
|-----|---------|
| **Predictions** (general beliefs about the future) | *I **will** probably work from home more in the future.* |
| **Promises** | *I **will** help you with your project, I promise.* |
| **Spontaneous decisions** (decisions made at the moment of speaking) | *The phone is ringing. I **will** answer it.* |
| **Offers and requests** | *I **will** carry your bag for you.* |

**Form:** will + base verb (no change for he/she/it)
- *I **will** call you tomorrow.*
- *She **will** be a great manager.*
- *They **will** arrive soon.*

**Negative:** will not = won't
- *I **won't** forget your birthday.*
- *He **won't** like this movie.*

**Questions:** Will + subject + base verb?
- ***Will** you come to the party?*
- ***Will** she pass the exam?*

#### *Going To* + base verb

| Use | Example |
|-----|---------|
| **Plans and intentions** (decisions already made) | *I **am going to** start a new job next month.* |
| **Predictions with evidence** (we can see something will happen) | *Look at those clouds! It **is going to** rain.* |

**Form:** am / is / are + going to + base verb
- *I **am going to** travel to Japan next year.*
- *She **is going to** study medicine.*
- *They **are going to** buy a house.*

**Negative:** am not / isn't / aren't + going to
- *I am **not going to** attend the meeting.*
- *He **isn't going to** apply for that job.*

**Questions:** Am / Is / Are + subject + going to + base verb?
- ***Are** you **going to** call him?*
- ***Is** she **going to** join us for dinner?*

#### Present Continuous for Future (Fixed Arrangements)

We use present continuous for arrangements that are already planned, often with a specific time or date.

- *I **am meeting** my boss tomorrow at 10 am.*
- *She **is flying** to London next Monday.*
- *We **are having** dinner with friends on Saturday.*

| Form | Use | Example |
|------|-----|---------|
| **going to** | plan or intention (you decided before) | *I am going to look for a new job.* |
| **present continuous** | fixed arrangement (time and place are set) | *I am having a job interview on Friday at 3 pm.* |
| **will** | spontaneous decision or promise | *I will help you with your CV.* |

> ### Common Mistakes
>
> ❌ *I will going to visit my family next week.*
> ✅ *I am going to visit my family next week.* (don't combine *will* and *going to*)
>
> ❌ *She will to call you later.*
> ✅ *She will call you later.* (no *to* after *will*)
>
> ❌ *I am going to buy a car. I decided right now.*
> ✅ *I will buy this car.* (spontaneous decision → *will*)
> ✅ *I am going to buy a car next year.* (plan → *going to*)
>
> ❌ *Look at that car! It will crash!*
> ✅ *Look at that car! It is going to crash!* (prediction with evidence → *going to*)

## Grammar

### First Conditional: Real Future Possibility

We use the **first conditional** to talk about a real or possible situation in the future and its result.

**Form:** If + present simple, will + base verb

| If clause (condition) | Main clause (result) |
|-----------------------|----------------------|
| **If** you study hard, | you **will pass** the exam. |
| **If** it rains, | I **will stay** home. |
| **If** she gets the job, | she **will move** to London. |

The order can change. When the *if* clause comes first, use a comma. When the main clause comes first, no comma.

- ***If** you save money, you **will be able to** travel.*
- *You **will be able to** travel **if** you save money.*

**Negative:** Use *if + present simple negative* or *won't* in the main clause.

- ***If** you **don't** study, you **won't pass** the exam.*
- *I **won't go** out **if** it **rains**.*

**Questions:** *What will you do **if** you don't get the job?*

#### Variations of the First Conditional

| Variation | Meaning | Example |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| **unless** + present simple | = if not | *You won't pass **unless** you study.* (= if you don't study) |
| **as long as** + present simple | = only if / provided that | *You can borrow my car **as long as** you drive carefully.* |
| **if** + present simple, **imperative** | instruction or advice | ***If** you feel tired, **take** a break.* |

- ***Unless** you hurry, you will miss the bus.*
- *You can stay here **as long as** you pay rent.*
- ***If** you need help, **call** me.*

### Modal Verbs for Advice

| Modal Verb | Strength | Example |
|------------|----------|---------|
| **should** | strong advice | *You **should** apply for that scholarship.* |
| **shouldn't** | strong negative advice | *You **shouldn't** quit your job without a plan.* |
| **ought to** | strong advice (slightly more formal) | *You **ought to** see a doctor about that pain.* |
| **could** | a suggestion (one possibility) | *You **could** try a different approach.* |
| **might want to** | a gentle suggestion | *You **might want to** check the price first.* |

**If I were you, I would...** — This is a very common and friendly way to give advice.

- ***If I were you, I would** accept the job offer.*
- ***If I were you, I wouldn't** worry about it too much.*

> ### Common Mistakes
>
> ❌ *If I will have time, I will help you.*
> ✅ *If I have time, I will help you.* (use present simple in the *if* clause, not *will*)
>
> ❌ *If I would be you, I would study more.*
> ✅ *If I were you, I would study more.* (use *were*, not *would be*)
>
> ❌ *You should to talk to your manager.*
> ✅ *You should talk to your manager.* (no *to* after *should*)
>
> ❌ *Unless you will study, you won't pass.*
> ✅ *Unless you study, you won't pass.* (present simple after *unless*)
>
> ❌ *I recommend you to apply for that job.*
> ✅ *I recommend applying for that job.* OR *I recommend that you apply for that job.*

## Pronunciation

### Sentence Stress for Emphasis in Advice

When giving advice, we often stress the advice word to make it stronger or more convincing.

**Stress the advice verb (*should, could, recommend, advise*):**

- You **SHOULD** talk to your manager.
- You **SHOULDN'T** worry too much.
- I **REALLY** think you should apply.
- I **STRONGLY** recommend taking a break.

**Stress the word *were* in *If I were you*:**

- If I **WERE** you, I would accept the offer.
- If I **WERE** you, I wouldn't do that.

**Stress the result in conditional sentences:**

- If you study hard, you **WILL** pass.
- If you don't hurry, you **WILL** miss the bus.
- If you save money, you **CAN** travel.

### Grammar: Second Conditional

**Form**

We use the second conditional to talk about imaginary or unreal situations in the present or future.

> **If + past simple, would + base verb**

| If clause (condition) | Main clause (result) |
|-----------------------|----------------------|
| If I had more money, | I would buy an electric car. |
| If everyone recycled, | there would be less waste. |
| If we lived near the beach, | we would swim every day. |

**Use**

We use the second conditional for:

1. **Imaginary situations** — things that are not true or unlikely to happen.
   - *If I were the president, I would stop pollution.* (I am not the president.)
   - *If we had a garden, we would grow our own vegetables.* (We don't have a garden.)

2. **Giving advice** — "If I were you..."
   - *If I were you, I would start recycling.*
   - *If I were you, I would use less plastic.*

**First Conditional vs. Second Conditional**

| First Conditional (real/possible) | Second Conditional (unreal/imaginary) |
|------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| If it rains, I will take an umbrella. (possible) | If it rained, I would stay home. (unlikely/imaginary) |
| If you study, you will pass. (real possibility) | If I were a bird, I would fly. (imaginary) |
| If I have time, I will help you. (real) | If I had a million dollars, I would travel the world. (imaginary) |

> **Common Mistakes**
>
> ❌ *If I would have more time, I would exercise.*
> ✅ *If I had more time, I would exercise.*
>
> Do NOT use "would" in the if-clause. Use the past simple in the if-clause.

> ❌ *If I was you, I would recycle more.*
> ✅ *If I were you, I would recycle more.*
>
> Use "were" (not "was") after "If I..." in the second conditional.

### Grammar: Relative Clauses

**Defining Relative Clauses**

We use defining relative clauses to give essential information about a person, thing, or place. Without this information, the sentence would not be complete.

| Pronoun | Used for | Example |
|---------|----------|---------|
| **who** | people | The woman **who lives next door** is a doctor. |
| **which** | things and animals | The book **which is on the table** is mine. |
| **that** | people and things | The man **that called you** is my brother. / The car **that is parked outside** is new. |
| **where** | places | The restaurant **where we met** is now closed. |

**Omission of the Relative Pronoun**

When the relative pronoun is the **object** of the clause (not the subject), we can omit it.

- *The man **who** I met yesterday was very kind.* → *The man I met yesterday was very kind.*
- *The film **which** we watched was great.* → *The film we watched was great.*
- But we CANNOT omit the pronoun when it is the **subject**:
  - *The woman **who** lives next door is kind.* (cannot omit "who")

> **Common Mistakes**
>
> ❌ *The person which called you is here.*
> ✅ *The person who called you is here.*
>
> Use "who" (not "which") for people.

> ❌ *That's the house who I grew up in.*
> ✅ *That's the house where I grew up.* / *That's the house which I grew up in.*
>
> Use "where" for places.

### Grammar: Passive Voice

**Present Simple Passive**

> **am / is / are + past participle**

| Active | Passive |
|--------|---------|
| The journalist writes the article. | The article **is written** by the journalist. |
| People speak English here. | English **is spoken** here. |
| The police arrest the suspect. | The suspect **is arrested** by the police. |

**Past Simple Passive**

> **was / were + past participle**

| Active | Passive |
|--------|---------|
| The reporter wrote the story. | The story **was written** by the reporter. |
| Someone stole my bag. | My bag **was stolen**. |
| The police arrested the thief. | The thief **was arrested** by the police. |

**When to Use the Passive Voice**

We use the passive voice when:

1. **The agent (doer) is unknown.**
   - *My phone was stolen.* (We don't know who stole it.)

2. **The agent is obvious or not important.**
   - *The suspect was arrested last night.* (It is obvious the police did it.)

3. **We want to focus on the action or the result, not the person.**
   - *The new hospital was opened in March.* (The hospital is more important than who opened it.)

**By + Agent**

If we want to mention who did the action, we use **by**:

- *The article **was written** by a famous journalist.*
- *The window **was broken** by the storm.*

> **Common Mistakes**
>
> ❌ *The window was broke.*
> ✅ *The window was broken.*
>
> Use the past participle, not the past simple.

> ❌ *The news is wrote by journalists.*
> ✅ *The news is written by journalists.*
>
> Irregular past participles must be learned.

> ❌ *The man was arrested from the police.*
> ✅ *The man was arrested by the police.*
>
> Use "by" (not "from") to mention the agent.

### Pronunciation: Review

This unit reviews key pronunciation features from the course.

**1. Sentence Stress**

In English sentences, content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives) are stressed, and grammar words (prepositions, articles) are unstressed.

- *I **met** my **friend** at the **park** **yesterday**.*
- *She **studies** **English** **every** **day**.*

**2. Weak Forms**

Many common words have weak forms when they are not stressed:
- *I can /kən/ swim.* (not /kæn/)
- *I would /wəd/ help.* (not /wʊd/)
- *There is /ðəz/ a problem.* (not /ðiː ɪz/)

**3. Contractions**

Contractions are very common in spoken English:
- *I am* → **I'm**
- *I have* → **I've**
- *I would / I had* → **I'd**
- *She is / She has* → **She's**
- *Do not* → **Don't**
- *Will not* → **Won't**

**I. Error Correction**

1. ❌ If I **would have** more money, I would buy a new car.
   ✅ If I **had** more money, I would buy a new car.
   *(Do not use "would" in the if-clause of the second conditional.)*

2. ❌ The woman **which** lives next door is a doctor.
   ✅ The woman **who / that** lives next door is a doctor.
   *(Use "who" or "that" for people, not "which".)*

3. ❌ The window was **broke** by the children.
   ✅ The window was **broken** by the children.
   *(Use the past participle "broken", not the past simple "broke".)*

4. ❌ She **has been** to Paris last year.
   ✅ She **went** to Paris last year.
   *(Use past simple, not present perfect, for a finished time like "last year".)*

5. ❌ He **don't** like coffee.
   ✅ He **doesn't** like coffee.
   *(Use "doesn't" (does not) for third person singular.)*

6. ❌ This is **the more expensive** restaurant in the city.
   ✅ This is **the most expensive** restaurant in the city.
   *(Use "the most" for superlatives of long adjectives.)*
